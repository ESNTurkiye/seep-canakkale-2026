# Every scene is entered — including the opening, but not the closing

Two choreographies were on the table for the opening screen. Either the viewer begins inside the artwork and scrolls backward out of it until the frame and the gallery appear, or the viewer begins with a small framed painting on a wall and scrolls into it. The opening originally used the first: the strongest opening available, with the pull-back turning the museum into a discovery rather than an announcement. That reasoning traded away something the museum device needs: a visitor's first screen was the myth already alive, before the museum around it had been established.

The opening now grows instead of receding. A visitor's first screen is the small painting hanging on the wall — frame, label, gallery wall all present — and scrolling grows it until it fills the view at full bleed, handing off into the "This Year" scene. This is a deliberate reversal (issue #14): establish "you are in a museum" before establishing "the museum is alive," rather than the other way around. The opening now uses the same growth every later scene uses to be entered, just without receding back afterward — it has nowhere to recede to; it hands off to the next scene instead.

Every scene after the opening is entered the way it always was. The viewer approaches a painting hanging on the wall, it grows until it fills the view, and it recedes as they pass. That is what walking a gallery is, and it is the rule the opening now also opens with rather than arriving at. The closing scene (issue #18) is the one deliberate exception: rather than being entered, it is *exited* — the visitor leaves the museum the way the pre-reversal opening used to begin, already inside the painting, receding out of it into the frame and the wall.

## Consequences

- **A cinematic scene costs roughly 320svh of scroll for a full approach-and-recede round trip.** The opening only grows one-way — no recede back to rest — so its track is shorter: roughly half that one-way distance plus a tail for the copy to fade in, an estimate to be remeasured against real content rather than a fixed budget line (see the comment in `OpeningScene.tsx`). Giving all nine scenes the full treatment is far more scroll than the site should ask for, so the budget stays three to four fully choreographed scenes — the opening, one in the middle, the closing — with the rest hung as paintings carrying only light movement.
- Which scenes earn the full treatment is a content decision, not a technical one, and can change without touching the mechanism.
- `prefers-reduced-motion` collapses every scene to its resting state: painting hung, frame visible, no scaling. The site must read completely in that mode. The opening's resting state already shows the frame and the copy together regardless of scroll direction, so this did not need to change with the reversal.
- At full bleed, the opening does not rest — it hands off to the next scene via the ink-crossfade transition (issue #15) rather than receding back to a resting size the way later scenes recede once the viewer has passed them.
- **The opening's growth now settles at peak (`openingPeakProgress`) before the track ends**, rather than reaching peak exactly at the last pixel of scroll. This leaves a real hold on the full-bleed frame — headline fully visible, nothing changing — before the ink crossfade (`openingInkStart`) begins, so the fade reads as a deliberate transition rather than interrupting still-settling motion.
- **The ink crossfade (issue #15) is a generic primitive** (`inkCrossfadeOpacity` in `lib/choreography.ts`, rendered by `<InkCrossfade>`): an opacity ramp from 0 to 1 through `var(--ink)`, driven by whatever scroll progress the calling scene already tracks. It is not a field on `OpeningState`/`EnteringState` — any scene that can reach and hold a peak can adopt it by passing its own `scrollYProgress` and a `start` threshold. It collapses to permanently transparent under `prefers-reduced-motion`, which is already a hard cut (scenes are stacked with no scroll-driven animation), so no separate reduced-motion fade logic is needed.
- **The "Why Çanakkale" → "Daytime" handoff uses it too, as of #17.** `EnteringScene` normally does an approach-and-recede round trip and never reaches-and-holds a peak (`enteringChoreography(1, peak, false).scale` is `1`, not `peak`) — fading to ink mid-recede would read as broken, not as a transition. `content/scenes.ts`'s `recede: false` opts a scene out of the recede: `enteringChoreography` grows to `enteringPeakProgress` and holds instead, and `EnteringScene` renders `<InkCrossfade>` from `enteringInkStart` the same way `OpeningScene` does from `openingInkStart`. Every other cinematic scene still recedes; which ones don't is a `content/scenes.ts` decision, not a mechanism one.
- **A hung, non-cinematic painting can reveal a real photograph as the viewer scrolls past it (issue #19).** `HungPainting.tsx` tracks a second, independent scroll progress on the same element — distinct from the entrance track that drives `lightMovement` — spanning roughly from the painting settling into view to it having fully scrolled past, and ramps a second, absolutely-positioned image layer in over the later portion of that track (`realPhotoReveal` in `lib/choreography.ts`). Reusing `lightMovement`'s own entrance track would front-load the reveal into the painting's arrival rather than firing once the viewer has genuinely scrolled past it, so this is a second, purpose-built track rather than a second consumer of the first. Gated on file presence via `realPhotoAvailable()` (`lib/availability.ts`), exactly like every other generated asset — a scene renders exactly as it does today until the real photo lands. A pure function of scroll position like everything else in this file, so it is fully reversible with no separate "already revealed" state; reduced motion holds it fully transparent, the same resting-state contract every other choreography in this file honours.
- **The real-photo reveal (#19) was never scoped to single-artwork scenes, and #20 relies on that.** `app/page.tsx` computes `realPhotoAvailability` over every scene's artworks unconditionally and threads it into `GalleryScene`, which handles both `artwork` and `wall` kinds identically — the only branch is `cinematic`. Each `HungPainting` on a wall gets its own `ref` and `useScroll` tracks, so the reveal is independent per artwork with no extra wiring, whatever the count. Issue #20 (the multi-venue walls "Daytime" and "Evenings") therefore needed no mechanism change, only the `-real.jpg` files themselves landing under the naming convention `realPhotoSrc()` already establishes — see `docs/art-direction.md`'s venue-reveal section.
- **The closing scene (#18) reverses the opening's own reversal, deliberately.** It is the last wall in the museum, with nothing to hand off to — so instead of being entered (hung small, grown on approach, per #14) it arrives already at full bleed and only recedes down to its hung, resting size, where it holds. This is the *pre-reversal* opening's shape (see this file's own git history from before #14), not a flag on `enteringChoreography`: a dedicated `closingChoreography` and `ClosingScene` component render it, since it shares `EnteringScene`'s `Painting variant="entering"` and general track/stage/wall shell but inverts the direction of every ramp and drops `copyOpacity` entirely — the closing scene's headline, body and CTA are shown throughout rather than gated behind reaching a peak, since (unlike every #16-gated cinematic scene) it has no approach phase to hide them during. `closingRestProgress` plays the same role as `openingPeakProgress`/`enteringPeakProgress` — the point past which the scene stops moving and holds — but there is no ink crossfade afterward: the Footer simply follows underneath in ordinary document flow once the track ends.
