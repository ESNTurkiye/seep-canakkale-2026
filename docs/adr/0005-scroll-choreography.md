# The opening recedes; every later scene is entered

Two choreographies were on the table. Either the viewer begins inside the artwork and scrolls backwards out of it until the frame and the gallery appear, or the viewer begins with a small framed painting on a wall and scrolls into it. We use both, in that order, and the order is the point.

The opening recedes. A visitor's first screen is the artwork at full bleed with the headline over it — the strongest opening available, and the pull-back turns the museum into a discovery rather than an announcement. Beginning with a small frame on a dark wall would spend the first screen mostly on empty wall, which is weakest exactly where it matters most, and on a phone it is close to nothing.

Every scene after it is entered. The viewer approaches a painting hanging on the wall, it grows until it fills the view, and it recedes as they pass. That is what walking a gallery is, and it is the rule the opening exists to establish.

## Consequences

- **A cinematic scene costs roughly 320svh of scroll.** Giving all nine that treatment is about thirty screens of scrolling, and the call to action lives at the bottom. So the budget is three to four fully choreographed scenes — the opening, one in the middle, the closing — with the rest hung as paintings carrying only light movement.
- Which scenes earn the full treatment is a content decision, not a technical one, and can change without touching the mechanism.
- `prefers-reduced-motion` collapses every scene to its resting state: painting hung, frame visible, no scaling. The site must read completely in that mode.
